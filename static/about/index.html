How this voice bot is built

A one-day build with a clear goal: when someone asks a question, they should hear Yash's answer in about a second, and it should be true.

How a question becomes speech

  1. You speak or type. Tapping the button records audio in the browser. Typing and the sample questions skip straight to step 3.
  2. Audio becomes text. The recording goes to the server, which sends it to Whisper on Groq and returns the words in about a third of a second.
  3. The model starts answering. The server sends the question, with Yash's whole profile already loaded in the prompt, to Claude Haiku on Amazon Bedrock and streams the reply back word by word.
  4. Words are cut into sentences. As soon as the first sentence is complete it goes to Amazon Polly, without waiting for the rest of the answer.
  5. Sentences become audio, in order. Up to three sentences are voiced in parallel, but the server releases them strictly in order.
  6. The browser plays one clip at a time. Text and audio arrive on one stream. A queue in the page plays the clips back to back, so voices never overlap.

Design decisions

Each one lists what I chose, why, and what I turned down.

The whole profile lives in the prompt, not behind search

Yash's profile is about 2,500 words, roughly 4,700 tokens with the instructions. That is small enough to hand to the model every time, and Bedrock's prompt caching makes the repeat cost and delay negligible (52 of 54 test turns hit the cache). The model never misses a fact because a search returned the wrong passage.

Turned down: retrieval (RAG). It adds a hop, a new way to fail and a tuning job, and only pays off when the knowledge is many times larger.

Stream everything, one sentence at a time

The slowest way to build this is to wait for the full answer, then voice it, then play it. Instead the model's output is cut at sentence ends and each sentence is voiced as soon as it exists, so the first sentence is playing while the rest is still being written. That is what gets first audio to about a second.

Turned down: voicing the complete answer at the end. It would add 2 to 4 seconds on every turn.

Tap to talk, not an open microphone

The bot listens, then answers, then listens again. That avoids echo cancellation, detecting when you interrupt, and the bot hearing itself. A Stop button and "Ask something else" still let you cut in at any time.

Turned down: a full two-way call. It is the better experience and the first thing I'd build next (see below), but it is a project of its own.

Record, then transcribe, instead of live browser dictation

Recording audio works in every modern browser and gives Whisper the whole sentence, which handles Indian English and technical terms well. A short list of words (Bedrock, OpenSearch, LoRA and others) is passed to Whisper to improve its spelling of them.

Turned down: the browser's built-in speech recognition. It is missing in Firefox and uneven in Safari, which would break the experience for some testers.

No spoken filler while it thinks

My first version played a short pre-recorded clip ("Hmm, okay.") when the answer was slow. In testing the clips sounded unrelated to the question, like random noise, so I removed them. While the answer is on its way the page now says "Yash is thinking." and the voice line moves, so the wait reads as work in progress rather than as a stuck page.

Turned down: canned clips (built and removed, see above), and for now a contextual filler written by a second model. That option is described just below.

An option I'd add next: a filler written by a second, tiny model

When a question arrives, a very small, fast model would write a three to six word acknowledgement that fits it ("Good one, let me think about my strengths."), in parallel with the real answer. It would be spoken only if it is ready before the real answer and dropped otherwise, and the real answer would never repeat it.

The trade-offs: it costs one extra tiny model call and one short voice clip per turn, well under a tenth of a cent. It must stay generic about the topic and never state facts, otherwise it could promise something the answer doesn't deliver. And it needs a rule for racing two streams. It is the first thing I'd try if the wait under the more natural voice feels too long.

Not built, because with this wait a related one-liner has to be right every time, and silence with a clear "thinking" state is safer than a wrong or random sentence.

A fast model with a strict script

Claude Haiku 4.5 starts streaming in about half a second and writes in a natural voice. The quality comes from the profile and the rules around it, not from a bigger model. If Haiku fails twice, the server falls back to Amazon Nova Micro rather than going silent.

Turned down: a larger model. Slower, higher cost and no visible gain on short spoken answers.

A natural Indian-English voice, paced by measurement

The voice is Arjun, one of Amazon Polly's generative voices, chosen after listening to ten candidates. Generative voices sound more natural than the older neural ones but take about a second longer to produce each sentence, and they are only offered in the US East region, so the voice service runs there while everything else runs in Mumbai. The server also sends speech markup that slows the voice slightly and adds a short pause after commas and a longer one after each sentence, which brings real answers to about 150 words a minute, close to a relaxed interview. The numbers are settings, so they can be tuned without code changes.

Turned down: the neural voice Matthew, which is about a second faster per answer (about 1 s to first audio instead of about 2 s). It is a three-line change back if speed matters more than naturalness, and a paid voice service with a small free tier that testers could use up.

One HTTP stream, no special infrastructure

The browser sends one request and gets one Server-Sent Events stream back, carrying text, audio chunks and a final summary. It works through ordinary proxies and hosts, needs no sockets and no session state, so the server stays stateless: the page sends the last few exchanges with each question.

Turned down: WebSockets, which not every host or proxy handles well, and which aren't needed while the conversation is turn by turn.

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no build step

The page is a handful of small files with no framework. It loads fast on a phone, can be read and changed by anyone, and fonts are bundled so it makes no third-party requests. The one distinctive element is the voice line, which reacts to the real audio levels of your voice and of Yash's.

Turned down: a framework and bundler. They add weight and moving parts to a page with one screen.

Keeping the answers true

A voice that sounds confident makes mistakes more convincing, so most of the effort went here rather than into the audio.

  • Only what's in the profile. The instructions tell the model to answer from the profile alone, to say so when it doesn't know, and never to invent stories, numbers or dates. It also avoids "this year" and similar words that would go stale.
  • Boundaries. Family, relationships, relocation promises, salary and similar topics get a polite deflection. If asked, the bot says plainly that it is an AI.
  • Rules the model kept breaking are enforced in code. It liked to start with "Sure," or "Ha," so a filter strips those openers. Answers over 120 words are cut at a sentence end.
  • A strict reviewer. A second, larger model grades each answer and must quote the exact lines of the profile that support every claim. Those quotes are checked against the real file, so it can't bluff.

The first rounds caught real problems: invented anecdotes, a claim of being unmarried, a commitment to relocate, and swapped figures. Each was fixed in the prompt or the profile and re-tested. Over 54 test turns (26 standard questions and 28 awkward ones) the average groundedness score was 4.73 and 4.82 out of 5, with no boundary violations.

Speed

Measured against the real services, mostly from a laptop. A quick check of the live site agreed: a typed question started speaking after about 1.6 s and a spoken one after about 3.1 s, including 0.2 s to turn the speech into text.

Two honest caveats. First, the full 54-question evaluation was run with the neural voice, and the Arjun figure comes from a spot check of three questions, so treat it as indicative. Second, 3 of those 54 turns were slow because the model stalled before its first word (twice for 7 to 9 seconds). I couldn't find the cause, so the server now gives up after 3.5 seconds and retries automatically.

Nothing is spoken during the wait. The status line says "Yash is thinking." and the voice line animates. The filler option above is how I'd shorten how long that feels.

When things fail

Each outside service can fail, and the visitor should always get something useful.

  • The model is slow or erroring: retry, then the backup model, then a pre-recorded apology.
  • The voice service fails on one sentence: that sentence is spoken by the browser's own voice.
  • Nothing was heard, or the recording was too short: a clear message and the cursor moves to the text box.
  • Microphone blocked or unavailable: the page switches to typing, and suggests Chrome or Safari if it is inside another app's browser.
  • Connection drops mid-answer: what arrived is played and a Retry button appears.
  • Too many requests: per-visitor limits protect the budget, with a friendly spoken and written message.

Privacy: audio is not stored. The text of each question is logged, along with timings and a one-way hash of the visitor's address, so I can see which topics the profile is missing.

How it's tested

  • 44 backend tests cover the sentence cutter, the retry and fallback logic, the opener filter, speech markup, the API and colour contrast.
  • 5 tests cover the browser's stream reader.
  • 57 browser tests run the real page in Chromium, Firefox and WebKit with a fake microphone and fake services: voice and typed flows, stopping and interrupting, every error above, history, phone layout, and keyboard use. They found real bugs, such as no feedback while the browser's microphone prompt was open.
  • An evaluation script runs the 54 questions against the real services and writes a report of speed and the reviewer's scores. I re-run it after every change to the prompt or profile.

What I'd change with more time

  • Make it a real conversation. Detect when you start talking over the bot and stop it, and stream your voice as you speak instead of uploading it at the end. That also removes the tap, and lets the page show captions live.
  • Find the model stalls. Log every first-word time in production, and if stalls persist, start a second request after 1.5 seconds and use whichever answers first.
  • Try the filler model. Build the contextual filler described under design decisions and measure whether visitors find it helpful or intrusive.
  • Share limits across servers. Rate limits live in one process's memory today. Running more than one server, or surviving restarts, needs a shared store.
  • Run the evaluation automatically on every change to the profile or prompt, and add a human spot-check of the reviewer model's scores.
  • Listen tests for the voice. I tuned the pace by measuring it and picked the voice by ear on my own. A proper A/B listening test with a few people would settle what sounds most natural.
  • Add monitoring and alerts for slow first audio, error rates and spend, rather than reading logs by hand.
  • Test on real iPhones and Androids over HTTPS once deployed, including in-app browsers.
  • Keep the source private. The repository is public and contains the profile. A private repository, or a profile loaded at deploy time, would fix that.

What else could be added

  • A speed control for the voice, and a captions toggle, so listeners can choose their own pace.
  • Hindi and other languages, with a voice for each.
  • Suggested follow-up questions after each answer.
  • A way to leave a message or book time with the real Yash when the bot reaches its limits.
  • A shareable transcript of a conversation.
  • A small dashboard of the questions people ask, to show where the profile has gaps.
  • More than one persona from the same code, since everything about Yash lives in a few text files.
  • An installable app icon for phones.

Known limits

  • It takes turns; you can't talk over it.
  • Speech recognition can mishear unusual names or heavy background noise. Typing always works.
  • It only knows what's in Yash's profile, and says so when asked about anything else.
  • Your history is kept in this browser only. Clearing site data or switching devices removes it.
  • Some model stalls are unexplained, as described under Speed.
  • It runs on one small server, so it suits a demo and a handful of simultaneous visitors, not a crowd.
